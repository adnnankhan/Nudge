03 · Problem statement

  1. 01

    Work stops

    The agent waits until the person comes back.

  2. 02

    The session can drop

    On Windows laptops, sleep drops the connection.

  3. 03

    Rushed decisions

    People approve without reading; work is redone.

  4. 04

    Juggling agents

    No single place shows which one is waiting.

The agent doesn’t fail. It waits.

And nobody’s there to answer. People who run AI coding agents, from professional developers to non-coders, step away while the agent works: a short break, a class or meeting, an overnight run. While they’re away, the agent stops to ask for permission or a question, and they don’t see it.

How might we make sure people never miss what their coding agents need, and can answer correctly and safely from wherever they are?

05 · Concept thinking

A remote for all your agents

Not just a way to say yes. One hub that shows what’s working, waiting and done, because people running agents need the whole picture, not one prompt at a time.

  • Built on the gapsBasic alerts and approvals already exist. The gaps were one place for every agent, plain-language risk, proof an answer arrived and a computer that stays awake.
  • Every feature answers a factFrom pairing by QR to voice replies, each kept feature traces back to the research.
  • New task: questioned, then keptA task often finishes while you’re away, and you want to start the next step without going back.
  • Cut: editing code and commit buttonsA phone is the wrong place to write code, and commits already arrive as approvals.
  • Later: history and trust rules“Always allow this in this project” needs real usage before it can be designed well.

06 · User flow

Every path, including failures

I mapped every path from sign-in to every feature, including what happens when things go wrong, because that’s where trust is won or lost.

  • Familiar sign-inGoogle or email and password, the two most familiar options.
  • A short explanation firstThe first step happens on the computer, so people need to know why before they install anything.
  • You confirm each agentThe helper finds agents automatically, and nothing connects without a yes.
  • Notifications, with a reasonAsked for right after pairing, the moment the value is obvious.
  • Keep-awake from the startOffered as soon as Home opens, so the session doesn’t drop on the first use.
  • Face ID or fingerprintApprove from anywhere. Typing needs the app open, because a typed answer deserves a full screen.
  • One queue per sessionSeveral requests are reviewed in order instead of hunting.
  • Stop confirms only when files changedAsking every time teaches people to tap through confirmations.
  • Undo, one step at a time“Go back further” is there too, so the common case is one tap and bigger rollbacks are a deliberate choice.
  • Limits explained, not hiddenCursor’s Stop button is shown with an explanation, so people understand the limit rather than wondering where the control went.
  • Nothing fails silentlyEvery failure gets one clear message and one next step, from a sleeping computer to a failed QR scan.
  • Take over from StopTapping Stop asks once, “This stops it in your terminal and continues from your phone”, instead of adding a Take over button.

08 · Wireframes

53 wireframes before any style

Every screen in the flow at mid-fidelity, so structure and flow were settled before style could distract from them.

  • Floating bottom navigationLike iOS apps use today. Content stays visible, and it’s reachable with one thumb.
  • Four items, not threeThree looked unfinished at the bottom of the screen.
  • Add a computer, in the dropdownThat’s where people look for their computers.
  • One more checkFor anything missing before moving on, because a missing screen costs minutes now and days after visual design.
  • Feasibility, checked againAgainst an app built on the same idea, so the design wouldn’t promise anything impossible. It confirmed Undo restores files only, Cursor can’t be controlled, and how to handle sessions started in the terminal.

09 · Moodboard

Most directions, rejected on purpose

I explored colour and type directions with Claude, because the first options were rarely good enough to build a product on.

  • Dated styles rejectedThe first set looked like styles from 2020, and a tool for AI agents has to feel current.
  • A mix of two directionsHeld to HCI and Google guidelines, contrast and spacing, so a style choice couldn’t turn into a usability problem later.
  • Calm minimalismModern effects and strong typography, because a screen you decide on in seconds has to stay calm. Dribbble references set the quality bar.
  • Correct isn’t enoughDirections that looked lacking were rejected even when they followed the rules. It has to feel premium.

Better by removing things

The first full design got a hard review. Most changes removed things, because every extra element competes with the one decision on screen.

  • Needs you, firstHome keeps Needs you → Working → Completed, because what needs you must always come first.
  • New task in the centreSettings moved to the top-right and New task to the centre of the tab bar, where the most-used action belongs.
  • No countdownsA ticking clock pushes people to approve without reading. Requests show “x min ago” and “Goes back to your computer after 10 min” instead.
  • Friction matches riskLow and medium: Approve, then Face ID or fingerprint. High: Review, then Hold to approve, because a dangerous command deserves a deliberate action.
  • Colour on the icon onlyAlerts never colour the text, so warnings stay readable and the screen stays calm.
  • Black and white summariesSummary boxes use icons, because colour is reserved for risk.
  • No grey backgroundIt made the whole app look unfinished.
  • Named NudgeA logo of rings opening outward, a signal reaching you. Blue was dropped for black and white, which leaves colour free to mean risk.
  • Dark first, light tooDark is the version I refined furthest in Figma. Every component exists in both modes, so either stays consistent.

13 · Feedback and iteration

Tested with people who use agents every day

They can tell what’s missing. A chat-based way to work with the agent was missing, and adding a new task felt complicated.

Chat, without becoming a chat app

Its job is still to get you the right decision fast.

  • Chat in three places onlyA Home card opens its chat, New task opens a chat after you pick an agent, and Tasks lists every task. Home stays the approval hub.
  • Patterns people already knowLike Claude and ChatGPT (Jakob’s Law): your messages on the right, the agent’s as plain text, tool activity as one collapsible line.
  • New task lost its formFilling fields was the friction people described. Your last project and computer are used automatically, shown as one line you can tap to change.
  • “Ask me”, not everythingThe agent comes to you only when you want it to: before every action, only before commands, or just when it’s done.
  • Nothing asks twiceI walked the flows as a frustrated user. New task remembers your choices, cards don’t repeat who and where, voice drops into the message box, an open app shows one banner, Home keeps your place, and one check covers low and medium approvals for 5 minutes.
